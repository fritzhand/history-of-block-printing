The Repeating Hand

Some 4,000 years ago, a king carved in soapstone wore a cloak covered in trefoils. Was it printed?

Nobody knows. This page follows the evidence for printing on cloth, from the Indus to Gujarat, Egypt, China, West Africa and Europe, and says plainly what is evidenced, what is reported, what has not been found, and what is imagined.

      About

      Method, limits and credits

      How the evidence is handled

      • CONFIRMED means the cited page states it: the quote shown is on that page, and tools/check-quotes.py re-checks it. For museum catalogue records it checks the record's own fields.
      • PENDING means a credible source is named but was not read. The Han printed gauze is one: the museum pages rate-limited this study.
      • DERIVED is this study's own reading, such as a midpoint year, an absence, or a comparison. The note says what it rests on.
      • Wikipedia is a lead, never a source. A photograph appears only if its record states an open licence.

      The 3D model and the video

      The Priest-King model is built procedurally from published photographs and a few published measurements (17.5 cm high, 11 cm wide). It is a measured interpretation, not a scan, and the printed-cloth surface is an illustration of a hypothesis. The moving image in the header is a render of that model; a reconstruction video is planned.

      Limits

      This is a first pass. About a dozen leads are still unread, and the Han silk, the 1686 French ban and the Met's essays on Indian block printing are among them. The map markers for places the sources give only loosely ("India", "Italy") are approximate.
